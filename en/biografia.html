The Mind Behind the Metal

The Philosophy of Volume and Space

Emanuel Nunes was born in Maputo, Mozambique, in 1968, the son of Portuguese parents — his mother a chemical analyst, his father a metallurgy technician and artist by choice. From this upbringing came his early interest in the arts and large-scale construction. In 1974, the family emigrated to Malawi, where Emanuel completed his primary education, arriving in Lisbon in 1977 at the age of nine.

His technical training began at the Lisbon Industrial School, where he became a professional in foundry technique. Between the ages of 16 and 18, he attended the School of Fine Arts as an assistant student, shaping his craft in the studios of great sculpture masters such as João Fragoso and Dorita Castelo Branco, a mentor he worked alongside for ten years, until the end of her life.

Between the ages of 18 and 21, he specialized in the lost-wax casting technique at Foundry K in Spain and at the Louvre Museum Foundry in France — in parallel, he spent five years at the National Civil Engineering Laboratory (LNEC). It was in this world that he identified as the artist's "third hand": the one that never appears, but transforms clay, plaster or drawing into any scale and material. In 1985 he founded Fundarte, his own foundry and large-scale restoration company, active until 2005, with ten employees and his own father among them.

Through his hands passed pieces such as the largest sculpture ever exhibited in Portimão — 12 meters tall and 20 tons — and the reproduction of the Goddess Kun Iam, 25 meters tall and 50 tons, a gift from the Portuguese government to Macau on the day the territory passed to Chinese administration. All his works, certified with the Fundarte stamp, can be found today in museums and public and private spaces across Portugal, Moscow, Turkey, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Madeira, the Azores, Switzerland, Spain and the United States.

In 2004, the death of his mother — the person who always understood, supported and believed in his talent — marked a turning point. It was this moment that led him to rethink his professional and personal journey.

In 2005, a trip to Brazil awakened an old desire: to become the author of his own sculptures. With the support of Portuguese friends living in the country, Emanuel began working in various fields in São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro and other states, postponing his return to Lisbon. In 2007, a work trip took him to Santa Catarina — he immediately identified with the people and culture and decided not to return to Portugal. For three years he lived in Rio do Sul, developing his new career as an independent sculptor.

In 2010, he moved to Balneário Camboriú and opened his own studio, beginning work on pieces for buildings, residences, squares, parks and gardens, while also participating in exhibitions throughout Brazil. In 2011, he obtained Brazilian citizenship and became vice-president of the Association of Visual Artists and Environmental Preservation (ACAPPMA).

In 2017, he lost his father, Eduardo Rafael — the great influence and motivator of his career as an artist, his mentor and childhood idol, with whom he spent hours as a child watching him paint the landscapes of the African wilderness. Today, with over three decades of experience, Emanuel keeps that technical and emotional legacy alive, applied to his own monumental creations in steel and metal.
